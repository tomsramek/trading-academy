A hardware wallet is basically a safe with a little window. You can pass a paper in to be signed, and a
signed paper comes out. But the key used for signing never leaves the safe – even if the office around it
is full of thieves.

<Illustration name="trustTheScreen" />

## How signing works

1. **An app on your computer or phone** prepares the transaction: to whom, how much and with what fee.
2. **The device** receives it and shows it on **its own screen**.
3. **You** check the address and amount and confirm with a button or a touch.
4. **The device** signs the transaction inside and sends back only the signature.
5. **The app** broadcasts the signed transaction to the network.

The private key was created inside the device and has never left it. Even an infected computer only gets
a signed transaction, not the key.

## The screen is the only source of truth

An infected computer can show a different address on its screen from the one it sends to the device. The
hardware wallet's screen, though, shows what is really being signed. So:

- always check the address and amount **on the device's screen**, not on the monitor,
- if anything differs, reject the transaction.

## The PIN and wiping

The device is protected by a PIN. After a set number of wrong attempts (3 on some models, 16 on others) it wipes itself – so a thief cannot keep
trying PINs forever. For you this means: without a backup of the seed phrase, a wiped device would mean
lost coins.

## The secure chip and physical attacks

Many models have a so-called <KeyTerm term="secure-element">secure element</KeyTerm>, a chip designed so
that secrets cannot be extracted from it even in a lab. For older models without one, security researchers
have shown that with physical access to the device and special equipment the seed phrase can be
recovered. The defence is a passphrase, which is the topic of lesson eight.

Trust in the manufacturer is another question. The device's software – the so-called <KeyTerm term="firmware">firmware</KeyTerm> – was written by the manufacturer, and you trust it not to send
the key anywhere. That is why open code that independent experts can check is valued.

<Callout
  type="warning"
  title="The device is the strongest link, the person the weakest"
>
  Hardware wallets are rarely broken in practice. People lose money mainly by
  revealing their seed phrase, signing a fraudulent transaction or losing their
  backup.
</Callout>

## Common myths

- **"If the computer is infected, a hardware wallet is useless."** It still protects the key – you just
  have to check the screen.
- **"A stolen wallet = stolen money."** Without the PIN and passphrase a thief normally cannot get to
  the money.
- **"I do not need to update the firmware."** Updates fix bugs – just install them from the official app.

<Callout title="Summary">

- A hardware wallet signs transactions inside; the key never leaves it.
- Check the address and amount on the device's screen – the computer monitor can lie.
- The PIN stops guessing; after wrong attempts the device wipes itself, which is why you need a backup.
- A secure chip makes physical attacks harder; trust in the firmware rests on the manufacturer and open
  code.

</Callout>

Most losses when sending do not come from hacks but from mistakes: a wrong address, a wrong network, a
substituted transaction. The good news is that one habit helps against all three – look at the device's
screen and do not rush.

<Illustration name="addressCompare" />

## Receiving

1. In the app, display your receiving address.
2. Choose **show on device** and compare the address on its screen with the one on your monitor.
3. Only then send it to whoever is paying you.

An infected computer could show you an attacker's address. The device's screen shows your real address.

## Sending

- **Take the address from the source**, not from your transaction history. An attack called <KeyTerm term="address-poisoning">address poisoning</KeyTerm> means someone sends you a tiny amount from
  an address whose beginning and end match the addresses you usually use.
- **Watch the clipboard.** Some malicious programs on a computer quietly swap a copied address for another.
  So check the address after pasting – and above all on the device's screen.
- **Check the network.** The same token may exist on several networks; sent over the wrong one, it may not
  be recoverable.
- **Test payment.** For a new recipient and for large amounts, send a small amount first and wait for
  confirmation. It costs one extra fee.

## Never sign blind

For a simple transfer the screen shows the address and amount. For more complex transactions, though –
for example in blockchain apps – some devices show only an incomprehensible string of characters. Signing
such a transaction is called <KeyTerm term="blind-signing">blind signing</KeyTerm>: you approve something
you cannot see.

In February 2025 the Bybit exchange lost about 1.5 billion dollars in Ether. The attackers tampered with
the interface, so the employees approving the transaction saw an ordinary transfer. In reality they
signed a change that handed the attackers control of the wallet. It was one of the biggest thefts in the
history of crypto.

What follows for you:

- keep blind signing **switched off** on the device and turn it on only rarely and deliberately,
- if you do not understand what the screen shows, **reject the transaction**,
- for blockchain apps, prefer devices and apps that display the transaction in readable form.

<Callout type="warning" title="Permission to spend tokens">
  On Ethereum and similar networks you give apps permission to spend your tokens
  – often without limit. Such a permission stays valid until you revoke it.
  Check them from time to time and revoke the ones you do not need.
</Callout>

## Common myths

- **"Checking the beginning and end of the address is enough."** That is exactly what address poisoning
  relies on. Check the middle too.
- **"I have a hardware wallet, I do not need to read anything."** It only protects you when you read its
  screen.
- **"A test payment is a wasted fee."** It is the cheapest insurance there is.

<Callout title="Summary">

- Always verify a receiving and sending address on the device's screen.
- Take the address from the source, not the history; check the network and send a test amount to a new
  recipient.
- Keep blind signing off – the Bybit hack showed where approving what you cannot see leads.
- On Ethereum and similar networks, check and revoke permissions to spend your tokens.

</Callout>

Attackers rarely break a hardware wallet. It is expensive and slow. It is far easier to persuade the owner
to hand over the keys – by email, a fake app, a phone call or, in the worst case, by force.

## Phishing after data leaks

Wallet manufacturers and the shops that sell for them have had customer data leaks:

- **Ledger (2020)** – the names, phone numbers and postal addresses of about 272,000 customers leaked.
- **Trezor (2024)** – attackers got into the customer support system and the contacts of about 66,000
  people.

The wallets themselves were not at risk. But people on the leaked lists received convincing emails and
text messages: "security incident, verify your seed phrase", "update your device at this link". The rule
from the whole course applies here too: **no honest party ever asks for your seed phrase, ever.**

When buying a wallet, consider delivery to a pickup point instead of your home address.

## Fake apps and websites

- **Fake apps** in app stores and fake browser extensions that look like the official ones.
- **Search ads** that lead to a copy of the manufacturer's website.

Defence: download apps only via a link from the official website; type the website's address by hand or
keep it in your bookmarks.

## SIM swap

In an attack called a <KeyTerm term="sim-swap">SIM swap</KeyTerm>, an attacker persuades your mobile
operator to assign your number to their SIM card. Your text messages then go to them – including codes
for resetting your exchange password.

- For two-factor authentication use **an app or a security key**, not SMS.
- Set a **PIN or password** with your operator for changes to your number.
- On an exchange, turn on the **withdrawal address whitelist** if it offers one.

## Physical extortion

The darkest form is violence. It is called a <KeyTerm term="wrench-attack">wrench attack</KeyTerm> – after
the joke that you cannot break the cryptography, but you can intimidate the owner. In January 2025 a
co-founder of Ledger was kidnapped in France, and the kidnappers demanded a ransom in crypto. Similar cases
became more frequent in the following months.

The defence rests mainly on staying low-key:

- **do not brag** – neither on social media nor in the pub,
- **do not show balances and addresses** linked to your name,
- keep large savings so they cannot be reached **instantly** – for example multisig with keys in different places, so moving them needs several places or people.

<Callout type="risk" title="Your safety matters more than money">
  Under direct threat, do not play the hero. Money can be lost; health and life
  cannot. Prevention – staying low-key and private – is far more effective than
  anything you do at the moment of an attack.
</Callout>

## Common myths

- **"The email is from the manufacturer, it has its logo and my name."** After data leaks, attackers have
  both.
- **"An SMS code is enough security."** A number can be moved to another SIM card.
- **"Nobody will come for me, I am not a millionaire."** Attackers often target whoever brags, not whoever
  has the most.

<Callout title="Summary">

- After manufacturers' data leaks, convincing phishing follows – no honest party asks for your seed phrase.
- Download apps only via the official website; watch out for ads and fake extensions.
- Against SIM swaps: 2FA via an app or key, a PIN with your operator, withdrawal address whitelists.
- Against physical extortion, staying low-key and keeping savings out of instant reach help most.

</Callout>

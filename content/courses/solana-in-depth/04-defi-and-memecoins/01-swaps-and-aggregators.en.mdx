On an exchange like Binance you buy from an order book – another person or company stands on the other
side. On a decentralised exchange there is often no counterparty at all. There is a pool with two piles of
tokens and a formula that calculates the price from them.

<Illustration name="shoppingRobot" />

## The liquidity pool

A decentralised exchange – a <KeyTerm term="dex">DEX</KeyTerm> – runs as a program on the blockchain. Most
swaps on Solana go through a so-called <KeyTerm term="amm">automated market maker</KeyTerm>: a pool holding
two tokens, such as SOL and USDC. Whoever wants SOL puts USDC into the pool and takes SOL out. In the simplest pools, the price follows a rule that **the product of the two piles stays the same**.

Example: the pool holds 1,000 SOL and 150,000 USDC – so the price is 150 USDC per SOL.

| Purchase for | You get   | Average price | How much more |
| ------------ | --------- | ------------- | ------------- |
| 150 USDC     | 0.999 SOL | 150.15        | 0.1%          |
| 1,500 USDC   | 9.90 SOL  | 151.50        | 1%            |
| 15,000 USDC  | 90.9 SOL  | 165.00        | 10%           |

_Sample pool, without fees._

The bigger the purchase relative to the pool, the worse the price. This is called price impact. With
little-known tokens and small pools, one larger purchase can push the price up by tens of percent – and a
sale can push it down just as much.

## The aggregator

The same token pair often sits in several pools on different exchanges. An aggregator (on Solana, for
example Jupiter, mentioned as an example) goes through all of them and splits the trade so the final price
is as good as possible. That is why most wallets use an aggregator directly.

## Slippage and the sandwich attack

Between sending a transaction and its execution, the price can move. That is why you set a slippage
tolerance – how much worse a price you are willing to accept.

- **Slippage too low** – the transaction fails in a moving market.
- **Slippage too high** – you invite bots. A so-called <KeyTerm term="sandwich-attack">sandwich attack</KeyTerm> works like this: a
  bot sees your transaction, buys just before you (the price jumps), you buy
  higher, right up to your slippage limit, and the bot sells immediately. You
  pay the difference.

A sensible default for big tokens is a fraction of a percent. Small tokens need more slippage, so the risk
is higher too. Some wallets and aggregators offer sandwich protection – turn it on.

<Callout type="warning" title="An aggregator does not check what you buy">
  An aggregator finds the best price even for a fake token with the same name.
  Always verify the mint address – lesson 13 shows how.
</Callout>

## Common myths

- **"The price on the chart is the price I will pay."** In a pool, the size of the purchase relative to the
  liquidity matters.
- **"High slippage = a guarantee the purchase goes through."** And an invitation for bots.
- **"A DEX has no fees."** Pools take a fee on every swap, plus the network fee and the priority fee.

<Callout title="Summary">

- A DEX on Solana usually trades through liquidity pools with a formula, not with a counterparty.
- A big purchase in a small pool makes the price much worse (price impact).
- An aggregator splits the trade across pools for the best price but does not check whether a token is
  genuine.
- Keep slippage sensibly low – high slippage invites sandwich attacks.

</Callout>

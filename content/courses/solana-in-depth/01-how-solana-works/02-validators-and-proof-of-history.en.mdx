Bitcoin works like a lottery: whoever first finds the right number writes the block. Solana is more like a
choir with a list of soloists. Everyone knows in advance who will sing, and the others just check that
they sing correctly.

## Proof of stake: more at stake, more votes

Solana uses <KeyTerm term="proof-of-stake">proof of stake</KeyTerm>. Validators have SOL locked up – their
own and SOL delegated by other holders. The more SOL stands behind a validator, the more weight its vote
has and the more often it may write blocks.

A validator votes on every block it considers valid. On Solana, votes are ordinary transactions and cost a
fee – about one SOL a day or more. That is one reason running a validator is expensive.

## A schedule for the whole epoch

Time on Solana is split into **slots**. Since the network launched, a slot lasted about 0.4 seconds. Since
August 2026 it has been shortened step by step (350, 300 and 250 milliseconds), and from 9 October 2026 it
is meant to be 0.2 seconds. Slots make up a so-called <KeyTerm term="epoch">epoch</KeyTerm> – 432,000
slots. That used to be about two days; with the shorter slots it is about one day.

For each epoch a leader schedule is calculated in advance: who may write a block when. Every leader gets
four slots in a row and then passes the baton to the next.

The upside: transactions can be sent straight to whoever will write next, and the network does not wait
for a lottery. The downside: the schedule is public, so everyone knows whom they could try to overload.

## Proof of History: a cryptographic clock

For thousands of computers around the world to agree on the order of transactions, they need a shared
time. Solana uses <KeyTerm term="proof-of-history">Proof of History</KeyTerm> for that: the leader keeps
computing a hash of the previous hash. Each step takes a moment and cannot be skipped, so the chain of
hashes proves that time passed between two events. Transactions are inserted into the chain, which gives
them an order.

So Proof of History is not a way to agree (proof of stake does that). It is the metronome everyone
follows.

## When a transaction is irreversible

- **Confirmed** – two thirds of the stake voted for the block. Usually within one to two seconds. Enough
  for everyday payments.
- **Finalised** – 31 more confirmed blocks sit on top of it. With the original 0.4-second slots that took
  about 13 seconds, with shorter slots proportionally less. After that the block cannot be reversed.

## Alpenglow: the next big change

The code of the **Alpenglow** upgrade has been on the main network since September 2026, but it is still
switched off. Activation is planned for autumn 2026; at the time of writing (October 2026) no exact date
had been confirmed. Alpenglow is meant to:

- replace both Proof of History and the current voting with a new protocol,
- remove vote transactions from blocks, freeing space for users' transactions,
- cut finality to about 0.1–0.15 seconds – confirmed and finalised will then mean the same.

Once Alpenglow is switched on, part of this lesson will describe history. The principle – stake sets the
weight of a vote – will stay.

## Common myths

- **"Proof of History replaces proof of stake."** No – PoH is the clock; stake decides what is valid.
- **"A transaction is done as soon as the block exists."** A block takes a fraction of a second; the
  transaction is irreversible only once finalised.
- **"Anyone with a laptop can be a validator."** You need a powerful server and money for voting.

- Solana uses proof of stake: more locked SOL = more voting weight and more blocks written.
- Slots are being cut from 0.4 to 0.2 seconds; an epoch has 432,000 slots and with shorter slots lasts

- Proof of History works as a cryptographic clock that sets the order of transactions.
- The Alpenglow upgrade (on the main network but still switched off) is meant to replace PoH and voting

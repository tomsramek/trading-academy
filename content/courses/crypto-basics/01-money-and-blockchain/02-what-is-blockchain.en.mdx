"<KeyTerm term="blockchain">Blockchain</KeyTerm>" sounds like something that needs a PhD in computer science. In reality it is mostly
**a notebook of payments**. A very cleverly kept one.

## A village that keeps a notebook

Imagine a small village where people pay with chickens, goats and eggs. There is no bank, and
nobody fully trusts anybody. So who owes what to whom?

The villagers come up with a simple idea: **everyone keeps the same notebook**. When Peter sends
Jane two chickens, he announces it on the village green and everyone writes it down. Nobody has
the "main" notebook – all copies are equal.

<SharedLedger />

What if George secretly writes down at night that Peter sent him twenty chickens? In the morning
the villagers compare notebooks: four agree, one does not. The village trusts the majority – and
George keeps the chickens he actually has.

<Illustration name="midnightEdit" />

That is the core of a blockchain: **a record of payments that every participant keeps**, and a
change only goes through if the majority agrees. In Bitcoin, the copies are kept not by
villagers but by thousands of computers around the world, called <KeyTerm term="node">nodes</KeyTerm>.

## Fingerprints: a tiny change, a completely different result

Comparing whole notebooks line by line would be slow. So a blockchain uses a <KeyTerm term="hash">fingerprint (hash)</KeyTerm> – a short "fingerprint" of any
text. Bitcoin uses a calculation called SHA-256.

Look what changing a single character does:

| Text                           | SHA-256 fingerprint (first 16 characters) |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| `Peter sends Jane 2 chickens`  | `d0be996747d8bbbb…`                       |
| `Peter sends Jane 20 chickens` | `caa639ad2df2413a…`                       |

The fingerprints are not even slightly alike. And that is exactly the trick:

- the same text **always gives the same fingerprint**,
- the smallest change gives **a completely different fingerprint**,
- you **cannot work back** from the fingerprint to the original text.

So comparing fingerprints is enough to see at once whether anything was changed. George's
little fraud would be caught in a fraction of a second.

## Blocks linked into a chain

Payments are not recorded one by one but in batches – **blocks**. Each block contains a list of
payments plus **the fingerprint of the previous block**. That creates a chain: block + chain,
blockchain.

<BlockchainDiagram />

If someone wanted to change a payment in an old block, that block's fingerprint would change.
But the fingerprint is stored in the next block – so that one no longer matches either. Nor does
the one after it. A fraudster would have to rewrite the whole rest of the chain before the rest
of the network noticed. For Bitcoin, that is practically impossible.

The first Bitcoin block was created on 3 January 2009, and the chain has only grown longer since.

## Why it works without a bank

Normally a bank confirms your payment – you trust it to keep its records right. A blockchain
replaces that trust with rules and maths: everyone has a copy, fingerprints reveal any change,
and the network decides on each new block, not a single company. This is called <KeyTerm term="decentralization">decentralization</KeyTerm>.

It does not come for free, though:

- **It is slower.** Getting thousands of computers to agree takes longer than one bank writing
  a line.
- **It costs energy and fees.** Someone has to create the blocks and wants to be paid for it
  (more in the lesson on mining).
- **Mistakes cannot be undone.** Send money to the wrong address and no customer hotline will
  bring it back.

<Callout type="warning" title="Blockchain ≠ good investment">
  A project "running on a blockchain" is not automatically safe or valuable. A
  blockchain only guarantees that a record cannot be quietly rewritten – not
  that it makes sense.
</Callout>

## Common myths

- **"Blockchain means Bitcoin."** Bitcoin is the first and best-known blockchain, but far from
  the only one.
- **"Everything on a blockchain is anonymous."** More like public: anyone can see every payment,
  just with addresses instead of names.
- **"A blockchain can't be hacked, so I can't lose my money."** The chain itself holds, but
  people lose money through stolen passwords, scams and mistakes – the whole Security module is
  about that.

<Callout title="Summary">

- A blockchain is a shared record of payments kept by every participant of the network.
- A fingerprint (hash) reveals even the smallest change.
- Blocks are linked by fingerprints, so an old record cannot be rewritten without rewriting everything after it.
- Rules and network agreement replace the bank – at the cost of speed, energy and mistakes that cannot be undone.

</Callout>

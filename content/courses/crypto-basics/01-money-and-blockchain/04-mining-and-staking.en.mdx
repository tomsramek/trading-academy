In the previous lessons we said that "the network" writes new payments into blocks. But the
network is thousands of computers that do not know each other and do not have to trust each other.
**Which of them may write the next block?** If anyone could, any time, George from the blockchain lesson
would write himself as many chickens as he liked.

The solution is simple and a little harsh: **writing a block has to cost something.** Whoever wants
to cheat must pay a lot first – and loses what they paid.

## Proof of work: a guessing race

Bitcoin uses <KeyTerm term="proof-of-work">proof of work</KeyTerm>. So-called miners compete to be
the first to find a number that makes the fingerprint (hash) of the new block start with enough
zeros. There is no clever shortcut – you can only guess. Fast, and a lot.

<Illustration name="miningRace" />

Whoever guesses first adds the block and gets a reward: **newly created bitcoins plus the fees**
from the payments in the block. Since April 2024 that is 3.125 BTC per block. That is why it is
called <KeyTerm term="mining">mining</KeyTerm> – new coins are "mined" from work, not from the ground.

To keep blocks coming roughly **every 10 minutes**, the network keeps adjusting how hard the
guessing is. More miners join, guessing gets harder; fewer miners, it gets easier.

**Why this stops fraud:** anyone who wanted to rewrite an old block would have to "guess" its
number again, plus the numbers of all blocks after it – faster than all other miners combined.
They would need most of the computing power of the whole network. For Bitcoin, that would cost an
astronomical amount.

The price for this is **electricity**. Bitcoin mining uses as much energy as a whole mid-sized
country – one of the most common criticisms of cryptocurrencies.

## Proof of stake: a deposit instead of electricity

The second approach is called <KeyTerm term="proof-of-stake">proof of stake</KeyTerm>. Instead of a
race of computers, **validators** lock up their own cryptocurrency as a deposit. The network picks
who writes the next block by lottery – the bigger the deposit, the bigger the chance.

A validator who cheats loses part of the deposit (this is called _slashing_). So cheating still
does not pay – you just risk your own money instead of electricity.

Ethereum switched from mining to proof of stake on **15 September 2022**, and according to
ethereum.org its energy use dropped by about **99.95%**. Running your own Ethereum validator
requires locking up at least 32 ETH.

|                      | Proof of work            | Proof of stake             |
| -------------------- | ------------------------ | -------------------------- |
| Who writes blocks    | miners                   | validators                 |
| What is at stake     | electricity and hardware | their own cryptocurrency   |
| Penalty for cheating | wasted energy            | losing part of the deposit |
| Example              | Bitcoin                  | Ethereum (since 2022)      |

## Staking for ordinary people

When an exchange or app talks about "<KeyTerm term="staking">staking</KeyTerm>", it usually means **you lend your cryptocurrency to
validators** and get part of their reward in return. It sounds like interest at a bank. It is not
interest at a bank.

<Callout type="risk" title="Staking is not a savings account">
  The reward is paid in a cryptocurrency whose price can drop sharply – a few
  percent a year will not save you if the price falls by a third. Deposits are
  often locked for some time, and the platform you stake through can go bust.
  Nobody guarantees you anything.
</Callout>

## Common myths

- **"Miners dig up bitcoin out of nothing, for free."** They pay for electricity and special
  hardware – and when the price drops, mining stops paying off.
- **"Proof of stake is less secure because it costs nothing."** It does cost – validators risk
  their own deposit instead of electricity.
- **"Staking is risk-free interest."** See the box above.

<Callout title="Summary">

- Writing a new block has to cost something, otherwise cheating would pay.
- Proof of work (Bitcoin): miners guess, the winner gets new coins and fees; it uses a lot of
  electricity.
- Proof of stake (Ethereum): validators lock up a deposit and lose it for cheating; energy use is
  a tiny fraction.
- Staking in an app means lending your cryptocurrency to validators – with a reward, but also
  with risks.

</Callout>

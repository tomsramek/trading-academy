Jane is selling her old bike to Tom, and he wants to pay in bitcoin. Jane sends him her address,
Tom clicks **Send** – and then stares in disbelief, because "nothing is happening". In fact, a lot
is happening.

## The journey of one payment

<TransactionFlow />

### 1. The signature

First, the wallet **signs** the payment with a <KeyTerm term="private-key">private key</KeyTerm> –
a secret number only Tom has. The signature proves the payment really comes from him, and nobody
can forge it or move it to another payment. (Keys and wallets get a lesson of their own.)

### 2. The waiting room

The signed payment is broadcast to the network and lands in the "waiting room" of unconfirmed
transactions – called the <KeyTerm term="mempool">mempool</KeyTerm>. There it waits until someone
writes it into a block.

### 3. Into a block

Bitcoin adds a new block **every 10 minutes on average**, Ethereum roughly **every 12 seconds**.
But not everything that is waiting fits into one block – and this is where fees come in.

### 4. Confirmations

Once the payment is in a block, it has its first <KeyTerm term="confirmation">confirmation</KeyTerm>.
Every further block on top adds another confirmation and makes reversing it ever more impossible.
For larger amounts, services often wait for several confirmations – for Bitcoin, six (about an
hour) is a common rule of thumb.

## Fees: an auction for bus seats

Imagine a bus that leaves every ten minutes and has a limited number of seats. There are more
passengers than seats, so the driver takes those who offer more first. Whoever offers little
waits for the next bus. Or the one after. Or until tomorrow.

That is exactly how fees work:

- **The fee goes to whoever writes the transaction into a block** – as a reward for the work.
- **The fee depends on demand**, not on how much you send. When many people want to send at once,
  fees go up.
- **Low fee = longer wait.** That is why wallets usually let you choose "slow and cheap" or "fast
  and pricier".

<Callout type="warning" title="Payments cannot be reversed">
  No bank, no refunds. Send money to the wrong address and it is gone. Always
  check the address (at least its beginning and end), and for a larger amount
  send a small test payment first.
</Callout>

## Common myths

- **"The more I send, the higher the fee."** Not with Bitcoin – the fee depends on the data size
  of the transaction and current demand. A million can cost the same as a hundred.
- **"Crypto payments are instant."** Sending is, confirming is not. Expect minutes, longer when the
  network is busy.
- **"I can cancel a payment."** Not once it is in a block. While it is still waiting, some wallets
  can replace it with a higher-fee payment – but do not count on it.

<Callout title="Summary">

- A payment is signed with a private key, broadcast to the network and waits in the mempool.
- Payments with higher fees get into blocks first; the fee depends on demand, not on the amount.
- Every further block adds a confirmation – more confirmations, more certainty.
- Nobody can reverse a confirmed payment, so always check the address.

</Callout>

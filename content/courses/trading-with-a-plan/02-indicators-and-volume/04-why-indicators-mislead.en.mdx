Indicators look like magic: one line crosses another and you know what to do. If it worked like that,
everyone who can open a chart would be rich. This lesson is about why they are not.

<Illustration name="crystalBall" />

## 1. Delay

Every indicator is calculated from prices that have already happened. A 200-day average needs a lot of
new prices before it turns. That is why the signal arrives after a large part of the move has happened
– you already know this from the lesson on moving averages.

## 2. False signals in a range

In a trend, trend-following indicators make sense. In a range, however, the price jumps back and forth
and the indicators with it. The result: one false signal after another, known as a _whipsaw_.

Bitcoin in 2024: from April to October the price moved in a range roughly between 49,000 and 73,000.

- **10 August** – a death cross at a price of 60,924, five days after the panic day.
- **28 October** – a golden cross at 69,962.

Anyone who sold and bought back on the crosses would have bought back 15% higher – plus fees.

## 3. Ten indicators, one piece of information

RSI, stochastic, MACD, CCI… Most indicators are calculated from the same thing: closing prices. When
they all agree, you do not have ten confirmations – you have one piece of information shown ten times.
More lines on a chart mostly mean more chances to find the one that agrees with what you already
think.

## 4. Overfitting to the past

On historical data you can find a setting that would have "made" a great profit. Just keep trying
average lengths and RSI thresholds until it works. This is called <KeyTerm term="overfitting">overfitting</KeyTerm>: the strategy learns random details of the past
that will not repeat.

How to protect yourself:

- **Simple rules** with few settings survive better than complex ones.
- **Test on data you did not use to invent the rules.** If a strategy only works on the period it was
  built on, it does not work.
- **Count fees and slippage.** Many strategies are profitable only on paper, without costs.

<Callout type="warning" title="There is no indicator that always works">
  If there were, everyone would use it – and it would stop working. Indicators
  are aids for orientation, not a money machine. Anyone selling you a "90%
  accurate indicator" is mostly selling a dream.
</Callout>

## What to use, then

A sensible base is small: **market structure**, **support and resistance zones**, one **trend filter**
(for example SMA 200) and **volume**. Add anything else only when you can say what it gives you on top.

## Common myths

- **"When five indicators agree, it is certain."** It is one piece of information five times.
- **"This strategy would have made 300% over the last year."** On the past, you can make anything.
- **"I just have not found the right setting yet."** Maybe because there is none.

<Callout title="Summary">

- Indicators are calculated from past prices, so they come late.
- In a range, trend indicators give false signals that cost money.
- Most indicators carry the same information – more lines do not mean more certainty.
- A strategy tuned to the past (overfitting) often fails in the future.

</Callout>

of November 2022 it had climbed from around 15,500 to more than 70,000.

## The top-down approach

Traders who use several timeframes usually go from the longer to the shorter one:

1. **The longer timeframe sets the direction.** On the weekly chart you find out whether the market is
   rising, falling or in a range.
2. **The middle timeframe shows the structure and zones.** On the daily chart you find the highs,
   lows, support and resistance.
3. **The shorter timeframe helps with timing.** On the hourly or four-hour chart you look for where
   exactly to enter and where to put the stop-loss.

A common rule of thumb: keep a ratio of roughly 1 : 4 to 1 : 7 between timeframes – for example a week,
a day and four hours.

## When timeframes disagree

A falling structure on the hourly chart, a rising one on the daily. Which one counts? **The longer
timeframe carries more weight.** In that situation a decline on the hourly chart is often just a
breather in an uptrend. A trade against the longer timeframe is a trade against the current – it can
work, but it needs stricter risk management.

<Callout
  type="warning"
  title="More charts = more noise, unless you have a method"
>
  Anyone who switches between the 1-minute, 5-minute and hourly chart without a
  plan will always find a "reason" for whatever they want to do right now.
  Choose your timeframes in advance and stick to them.
</Callout>

## Common myths

- **"It is falling on the minute chart, I am selling!"** First look at what the daily chart is doing.
- **"The shorter the timeframe, the more precise the entry, the more profit."** A shorter timeframe
  means more noise, more trades and more fees.
- **"Every timeframe has to give me the same signal."** It does not. The longer timeframe sets the
  direction, the shorter one only the timing.

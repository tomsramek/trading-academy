From the first course you know the <KeyTerm term="market-order">market order</KeyTerm> (right away,
at the best available price) and the <KeyTerm term="limit-order">limit order</KeyTerm> (only at your
price or better). With them you buy and sell. Protecting a trade and planning an exit, however, need
other order types.

## Stop orders: asleep until the price arrives

A stop order waits for a **trigger price**. Until the price reaches it, nothing happens. Then it wakes
up – and what it does next depends on the type:

| Order       | Once the trigger price is reached…        | Advantage                | Risk                                                       |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Stop-market | sends a market order                      | almost sure to fill      | in a fast market at a worse price (slippage)               |
| Stop-limit  | sends a limit order with your limit price | you know the worst price | if the price jumps past the limit, it does not fill at all |

A typical <KeyTerm term="stop-loss">stop-loss</KeyTerm> is a stop-market order. Imagine you have a
stop-loss at 62,000 and the market crashes like on 5 March 2024 – 14.5% in four hours. The stop-market
fills, say at 61,700. A <KeyTerm term="stop-limit">stop-limit</KeyTerm> with a limit of 61,900 may not
fill at all in such a drop – and you stay in the trade you wanted to close.

**For protecting against losses, a stop-market is usually better.** A worse price is a smaller evil
than no exit.

## OCO: one cancels the other

An <KeyTerm term="oco">OCO</KeyTerm> (_one cancels the other_) links two orders: when one fills, the
other is cancelled automatically. Typically a take-profit above the price and a stop-loss below it.
You do not have to watch which comes first – and you will not end up with a forgotten stop-loss left
hanging after the profit target filled, which later "throws you out" of a new position.

## Trailing stop: a stop-loss that moves with the price

A <KeyTerm term="trailing-stop">trailing stop</KeyTerm> has no fixed price but a distance – for example
5% below the highest price since the trade was opened. When the price rises, the stop moves up with
it. When it falls, the stop stays where it is.

Example: you buy at 60,000 with a 5% trailing stop. The stop is at 57,000. The price rises to 70,000 –
the stop moves to 66,500. Then the price falls to 66,500 and the order takes you out with a profit.

The weak spot: in crypto, swings of 5% are common even in the middle of a strong trend. A trailing
stop that is too tight will throw you out at the first bigger wave.

<Callout type="warning" title="Every exchange does it a little differently">
  The names, the available order types and what exactly happens when they
  trigger differ between exchanges. Before you use a new order type with real
  money, read the exchange's help pages and try it with a small amount or on a
  demo account.
</Callout>

## Common myths

- **"A stop-limit is better because I know the price."** You know the price – if it fills. In a crash
  it may not.
- **"A stop-loss guarantees an exact loss."** In a fast market the loss can be bigger.
- **"A 2% trailing stop is safe."** In crypto it will throw you out before you can blink.

<Callout title="Summary">

- Stop orders wait for a trigger price; a stop-market then sells at market, a stop-limit only up to its
  limit.
- For protecting against losses a stop-market is usually better – a worse price is a smaller evil than
  no exit.
- An OCO links a profit target and a stop-loss: one fills, the other disappears.
- A trailing stop moves with the price; in crypto it needs plenty of room.

</Callout>

Before you open an account with an exchange, you hand it your money and your documents. So choosing an
exchange is not about who has the nicer app or the bigger sign-up bonus. It is like choosing a bank – and
you do not choose a bank by the ad at the bus stop either.

## The MiCA licence

Since the end of 2024 the European Union has applied the regulation on markets in crypto-assets, <KeyTerm term="mica">MiCA</KeyTerm>
for short. Whoever wants to offer people in the EU buying, selling or storing crypto needs authorisation
from a supervisor – in the Czech Republic from the Czech National Bank, elsewhere from that country's
regulator. Firms that operated before had a transitional period. **It ended on 1 July 2026.** Since then
only licensed firms may offer these services to people in the EU.

## An example: Binance

One of the biggest exchanges in the world, Binance, did not get a MiCA licence. That is why, since
1 July 2026, it has not accepted new sign-ups or deposits from people in the EU and does not let them place new orders. Withdrawals stayed open so customers can move their money elsewhere.

The lesson for you is simple: **neither size nor a famous name replaces a licence.** Guides on the
internet that start with "sign up at …" can be useless half a year later.

## How to check a licence

1. **The national register** – your country's regulator (in the Czech Republic, the Czech National Bank)
   publishes a list of firms authorised to provide crypto-asset services.
2. **The ESMA register** – the European Securities and Markets Authority keeps a joint register of all
   MiCA-licensed firms in the EU. A licence from another EU country is valid in yours too.
3. **Compare the exact name of the firm.** An exchange often runs several companies in different
   countries. Check that the one you sign a contract with is the one holding the licence – you will find
   it in the terms and conditions.

It takes a few minutes and saves you a lot of trouble.

## What to look at besides the licence

- **Deposits and withdrawals in euros** – does it support SEPA bank transfers, or only cards?
- **Fees** – for trades, deposits and withdrawals. More in lessons 4 and 8.
- **The cryptocurrencies you want** – and the networks you can withdraw them over.
- **Withdrawals to your own wallet** – an exchange that does not let you withdraw crypto is not an
  exchange but a cage.
- **Your language and customer support** – handy when something goes wrong.
- **History** – has the exchange had data leaks or frozen withdrawals? How did it handle them?

<Callout type="warning" title="A licence is not insurance">
  A MiCA licence raises protection – the exchange must hold capital, keep
  customers' money separate and is supervised. But it does not mean deposit
  insurance as with a bank, and it does not protect you from falling prices.
</Callout>

## Common myths

- **"It is the biggest exchange in the world, it must be fine."** Binance showed that size does not
  replace a licence.
- **"They have an app in the App Store, so they are legal."** App stores do not check licences.
- **"Their licence is from Lithuania, it does not count here."** A MiCA licence from one EU country is
  valid throughout the EU.

<Callout title="Summary">

- Since 1 July 2026 only MiCA-licensed firms may offer crypto services to people in the EU.
- Check the licence in the national register or the ESMA register – by the exact company name from the
  contract.
- Binance has no licence, so it does not accept new EU customers or deposits.
- Besides the licence, look at fees, euro deposits, withdrawals to your own wallet and the exchange's
  history.

</Callout>
